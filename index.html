Guess the Share

How many people in the United States are immigrants, and who are they? Make seven guesses about today and the past. After each one, you’ll see the real number. An immigrant here means anyone living in the U.S. who was born in another country and wasn’t a U.S. citizen at birth.

Then and now: the immigrant share since 1850

Percentage of U.S. residents who were born in another country, from each census, 1850 to 2010, and the Census Bureau’s 2024 American Community Survey.

Today’s share isn’t new. Immigrants were 13% to 15% of the population in every census from 1860 to 1920. The share then fell for 50 years, to a low of 4.7% in 1970, and has climbed back since. In 2024 it matched the 1890 record of 14.8%.

Then and now: where immigrants were born

Percentage of all immigrants living in the U.S. in each year. In 1960, Africa is part of “Other.” In 2024, “Other” is Northern America and Oceania.

The share is similar to a century ago, but the people are not. In 1850, 92% of immigrants were born in Europe. In 1960 it was still 75%, and the largest group was from Italy (13% of all immigrants), followed by Germany, Canada, the United Kingdom, and Poland. In 2024, 10% were born in Europe, about half in Latin America, and almost a third in Asia. Mexico was the largest country of origin, at 22%.

The fuller picture

Almost everyone guesses too high. In a 2018 study, about 4,500 U.S.-born adults guessed, on average, that 36% of the U.S. population were immigrants. At the time, about 10% were legal immigrants and 13.5% were immigrants of any status. People in France, Germany, Italy, Sweden, and the United Kingdom overestimated too. The pattern held across political parties, education levels, and incomes.

It isn’t only immigrants. People tend to overestimate the size of small groups in general and underestimate large ones. Psychologists who study this argue it’s partly a quirk in how we turn hunches into percentages, and it shouldn’t be read only as fear or bias.

Who people think immigrants are matters more than how many. The same study found that the guessed number of immigrants didn’t predict much about people’s views. What did was what they believed about immigrants: how hard they work, how educated they are, and how much they rely on government help. People who personally knew an immigrant were more supportive of immigration.

The share is back near its historic high, and it recently fell. Immigrants were 14.8% of the population in 1890, 4.7% in 1970, and 14.8% again in 2024, according to the Census Bureau. Pew’s estimates, which use a different monthly survey, put the share at a record 15.8% in January 2025 and 15.4% by June 2025, the first decline since the 1960s. Many countries have higher shares; in Canada, about 22% of residents are immigrants.

Laws shaped both the share and the origins. In the 1920s, Congress set quotas by national origin that sharply limited immigration, especially from Southern and Eastern Europe, Asia, and Africa. The immigrant share fell for the next half century. In 1965, Congress ended the national-origin quotas, and immigration from Latin America and Asia grew. The shift from Europe to Latin America and Asia followed a change in policy, along with changes in the world economy.

Talk about it

  1. Which of your guesses was furthest off? Where did your picture come from?
  2. The study found that Americans living in areas with more immigrants overestimated more. Why might seeing immigrants in daily life lead to a higher guess for the whole country?
  3. Giving people the correct numbers barely changed their views on immigration policy. Telling them a story about one hardworking immigrant changed views more. What does that suggest about how people form opinions?
  4. How do news coverage and political speeches shape which immigrants come to mind first?
  5. Does it matter whether people know the real number? What decisions, personal or political, might depend on it?
  6. The immigrant share today is about what it was in 1890 and 1910. What was similar about how Americans reacted to immigration then and now? What’s different?

About the data

Actual figures: Pew Research Center’s analysis of Census Bureau data. The share of the population and of the labor force are for June 2025. The shares born in Mexico and without legal authorization are for 2023, the latest detailed estimates.

Then and now: Shares of the population from 1850 to 2010 and regions and countries of birth in 1960 are from the Census Bureau’s decennial censuses (2010 is from the American Community Survey). The 2024 share and regions of birth and the 1850 Europe figure are from a 2026 Census Bureau report. The shares from Italy (1960) and Mexico (2024) are from the Migration Policy Institute. The 2024 “Other” figure (2.2%) is what’s left after the four regions the Census Bureau reported.

What Americans guessed: Alesina, Miano and Stantcheva surveyed 4,500 U.S.-born adults in January and February 2018. Their study counted only legal immigrants, so its “actual” figures (10% of the population; about 10% of immigrants Muslim) are for legal immigrants in 2018. This page compares each guess with the figure that matches its question.
